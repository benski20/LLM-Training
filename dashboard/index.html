Letters become numbers

A computer can't read. Before a model sees any text, every character is swapped for an ID number. Each piece is called a token.

Your model's whole alphabet is the characters that appear in Shakespeare's plays: 65 of them, including space and newline.

Characters it has never seen (like emoji or é) don't exist for it. It can't read them or write them.

Guess the next letter

This is the only game a language model ever plays: read some text, then guess what comes next.

Play a few rounds against a simple model that learned by counting which letters follow which in Shakespeare.

When you're surprised by the answer, you learn something. Training is the model playing this game millions of times, adjusting itself a tiny bit after each surprise.

How much can it remember?

To guess well, a model needs to look back at what came before. Slide to change how many letters this counting model gets to look back at.

Watch the confusion score: how many letters it's effectively choosing between. Lower is better.

Something strange happens after about 4 letters: it gets worse. Longer snippets are mostly ones it has never seen before, and counting can't handle anything new.

A Transformer fixes this. It learns patterns instead of memorising exact snippets, so it can look back 128 letters and still make sense of text it has never seen.

Rolling the dice

      
A model never picks "the answer". It gives every possible next letter a probability, then rolls weighted dice. That's why you get a different answer each time you ask ChatGPT the same question.

      
Temperature changes the dice:

      
❄️ Low (0.2): it almost always picks the favourite. Safe, but it repeats itself.
🔥 High (1.8): unlikely letters get a real chance. Creative at first, then chaos.

Paying attention

      
"Attention" is the big idea behind Transformers. Before guessing, each word (or letter) looks back at everything earlier and decides which parts matter right now.

      
Nobody programs these choices. The model works them out during training, because paying attention to the right things makes its guesses less surprising.

Tiny vs. giant

      
You built the same kind of machine as ChatGPT. The difference is scale: how big the brain is, how much it reads, and how long it trains.

      
The bars use a "times 10" scale. Each grid step is 10 times bigger than the one before, otherwise your model would be invisible.

      
Numbers for other models are from their published papers and model cards. Many newer models don't publish theirs.
